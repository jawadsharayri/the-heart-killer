01 / CAUSES↗

How plaque begins

CAD usually develops as fatty plaque builds within the walls of the coronary arteries.

LDL cholesterolInflammationRisk factors
Open the detailed discussion ＋

What actually causes CAD?

The usual underlying process is atherosclerosis: plaque develops in the artery wall over many years. Coronary arteries are the vessels on the heart’s surface that supply the heart muscle itself. The artery has several tissue layers. The intima is the thin inner lining; behind it, the muscular media helps the vessel respond to changes in pressure; the outer layer supports and protects it.

Cholesterol is not mucus. Cholesterol is a waxy substance carried through the bloodstream in particles such as LDL. When LDL and other material are retained in the artery wall, the body’s immune response can create inflammation. Over time, cholesterol, fats, cells, connective tissue, and sometimes calcium make up a plaque. A fibrous cap may cover a softer lipid-rich core. Plaques vary: some are more stable, while others can rupture more easily.

Risk is a combination

Risk factors include high LDL cholesterol, high blood pressure, diabetes, smoking and secondhand smoke, low physical activity, and some eating and sleep patterns. Age, family history, and some health conditions also matter and cannot simply be changed. Factors may interact—for example, diabetes can occur alongside high blood pressure and cholesterol problems. Having a risk factor raises the chance of disease; it does not prove a person has CAD or that a heart attack will happen.

There is no single cause in every person. Clinicians use the whole picture—health history, measurements, symptoms, and sometimes tests—to assess risk.

Read the drawing: Gold is plaque within the wall; the dark center is the lumen. The plaque can narrow the channel, but the artery is still a living, layered tissue.

02 / SYMPTOMS♡

Listen to the signals

Some people notice no symptoms. Others feel chest discomfort when the heart needs more oxygen.

Chest pressureBreathlessnessFatigue
Open the detailed discussion ＋

Why symptoms happen

The heart needs more oxygen-rich blood when it works harder, such as during exercise or strong emotion. A narrowed coronary artery may not increase its blood supply enough. The mismatch between oxygen supply and the heart’s demand can cause angina—a symptom, not a disease by itself.

People often describe pressure, squeezing, heaviness, tightness, or discomfort in the chest. It may spread to the arm or shoulder, jaw, neck, back, or upper abdomen. Shortness of breath, nausea, cold sweat, dizziness, or unusual fatigue may occur. Symptoms can be mild, severe, brief, persistent, or absent. Some people first learn they have CAD after a heart event.

Patterns matter, but do not self-diagnose

Angina that repeatedly appears with a similar level of activity and improves with rest is sometimes called stable angina. New discomfort, discomfort at rest, a pattern that is becoming more frequent or intense, or symptoms that do not settle can signal an emergency. A person should not wait to see whether serious symptoms pass.

Age, diabetes, and individual differences can change how symptoms feel. Not everyone experiences the same “classic” chest pain. A clinician evaluates symptoms alongside history and tests.

Emergency: If you or someone nearby has new, severe, or persistent chest pressure, trouble breathing, faintness, or other possible heart attack symptoms, call your local emergency number now. Do not use this guide to decide whether it is safe to wait.

Further reading: Mayo Clinic · CDC · Cleveland Clinic.

03 / EFFECTS⌁

When flow is reduced

A narrowed artery may limit oxygen supply. A clot can sometimes block flow suddenly.

IschemiaBlood clotHeart attack
Open the detailed discussion ＋

From reduced flow to muscle stress

When the heart muscle does not receive enough oxygen for its needs, the condition is called ischemia. Temporary ischemia can cause angina. A narrowing may be present for years before symptoms appear, and the heart may receive enough blood at rest but not during exertion.

How a clot can change the story quickly

A plaque can crack or rupture, exposing material that prompts platelets and clotting proteins to gather. A thrombus (blood clot) can grow at that site. If it blocks a coronary artery, oxygen delivery downstream can fall sharply. A heart attack (myocardial infarction) occurs when part of the heart muscle is injured because blood flow is interrupted. Prompt emergency treatment can limit damage; time matters.

Possible longer-term effects

Injured or repeatedly oxygen-starved muscle may pump less effectively. In some people this contributes to heart failure, when the heart cannot pump enough blood for the body’s needs. Ischemia or heart muscle injury can also be associated with abnormal electrical rhythms. These complications are possible, not inevitable. The outcome depends on the location and extent of disease, other health conditions, and how quickly care is received.

Two pathways: Gradual narrowing can cause exertional angina; a sudden clot can produce an acute heart attack. A clinician distinguishes these possibilities.

Further reading: CDC · Cleveland Clinic · American Heart Association.

04 / TREATMENTS＋

Care that fits the person

Care may include healthy habits, medicines, or procedures chosen with a healthcare team.

MedicinesStentAtherectomy
Open the detailed discussion ＋

First: understand the person and the arteries

Care is chosen by the healthcare team with the patient. It depends on the symptoms, which vessels are affected, test findings, other conditions, expected benefits and risks, and the person’s preferences. Evaluation may include a health history and examination, blood tests, an ECG, an echocardiogram, stress testing, CT imaging, or coronary angiography. No single test is right for everyone.

Long-term risk management

Plans often combine lifestyle support with medicines. A clinician may prescribe cholesterol-lowering treatment such as a statin, blood-pressure medicines, anti-anginal medicines, or antiplatelet medicines when indicated. Each medicine has a specific purpose and potential side effects; a patient should ask the prescriber or pharmacist how and when to take it. Never start, stop, or change a medicine based on this website.

Procedures that restore blood flow

Angioplasty / PCI: a catheter carries a small balloon to a narrowed area. Inflating it pushes plaque toward the wall and widens the channel. A wire-mesh stent may be expanded there to help hold the artery open. A stent treats a particular narrowing; it does not remove the tendency to develop plaque elsewhere, so medicines and follow-up can remain important.

Atherectomy: in selected cases, a tool on a catheter modifies or removes some plaque, often when calcium makes a lesion hard to expand. The rotating crown, catheter shaft, guidewire, and small particles shown in the diagram are a simplified representation. Different atherectomy devices work in different ways. It is not routine for every blockage and is not a cure.

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG): a surgeon uses a blood vessel from elsewhere in the body to create a route around one or more blocked sections. Some people benefit more from medicines, some from PCI, some from surgery, and some from a combination.

Cardiac rehabilitation may support recovery and long-term habits after a heart event or procedure. It can include supervised activity, education, and support.

Important: This is a learning overview, not a treatment recommendation. A qualified healthcare professional explains which options fit a specific person.

Further reading: Mayo Clinic · American Heart Association · Cleveland Clinic.

05 / PREVENTION✳

Small steps, steady care

Everyday habits and regular check-ins can help lower risk and support heart health.

Move moreSkip tobaccoCheck in
Open the detailed discussion ＋

Prevention is a set of supports

No habit guarantees that a person will never develop CAD. But steady heart-healthy choices can lower risk and support care. Avoiding tobacco—including secondhand smoke—helps protect blood vessels. Regular movement supports blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, mood, and fitness. A balanced eating pattern that includes vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans, and other minimally processed foods can support heart health; local foods and a person’s needs matter.

Know the numbers with help

High blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar often do not cause obvious symptoms. Regular health visits can identify these risks before a person feels ill. A healthcare professional can explain which checks are appropriate based on age, family history, and health—not every test is needed by every person. If a family member had early heart disease, mention that history at a check-up.

Change what you can; get help with what you can’t

Some factors—such as age, family history, and certain medical conditions—are not under a person’s control. Access to safe places to be active, healthy food, and healthcare can also affect options. Prevention should not be framed as blame. A clinician, school nurse, trusted adult, or community health service can help identify practical next steps.

For people already diagnosed with CAD, prevention also means following the agreed care plan, taking prescribed medicines as directed, attending follow-up visits, and asking for help when symptoms or side effects change. Cardiac rehabilitation may be part of recovery for some people.

A realistic start: Choose one manageable habit to discuss with a trusted adult or clinician. Small steps can be more sustainable than trying to change everything at once.

Further reading: CDC · Mayo Clinic · American Heart Association.

THE SHORT VERSION

A little less room.
A lot to understand.

Healthy arteries give blood an open path. Plaque can narrow that path over time. If plaque breaks and a clot forms, the flow can change quickly.
